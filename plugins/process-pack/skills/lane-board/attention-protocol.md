# Attention Protocol (compact reference)

Applies whenever a lane, or the board as a whole, represents meaningful wait time for whoever is watching it. Grounded in decades of task-switching research: switching away from unfinished work leaves attention residue that degrades performance on return, even brief interruptions cost more than they appear to, and interruptions at natural boundaries cost far less than interruptions mid-task.

1. **Estimate before starting.** State a one-line duration estimate before beginning. Under about two minutes: say that waiting in place beats switching away. Two to ten minutes: suggest a low-effort filler, not deep work. Over about ten minutes: treat it as a scheduled block, not a wait — recommend stepping away fully.
2. **Front-load every question.** Ask everything needed before work starts. Don't interrupt mid-run unless proceeding would cause irreversible harm; non-blocking questions discovered mid-work get collected and presented at the end, not asked as they arise.
3. **Confirm the handoff.** Before starting, restate in one or two lines what will be done and what will be decided unilaterally along the way. This is what lets the person hand it off and stop actively thinking about it.
4. **Return with a ready-to-resume briefing.** After anything that took more than about five minutes, lead with, in order: where things stood when the task was handed off, what was done (outcomes only, not a process log), and the one decision now facing the person returning — before any further detail.
5. **Batch updates.** Don't stream progress pings. Consolidate multiple finished items into one digest, ordered by decision-urgency rather than completion order.
6. **Keep the queue.** When someone is juggling more than one active thread, hold the backlog for them: acknowledge a new idea that arrives mid-task, log it, steer back to the current thread, and flag it plainly if the number of simultaneously active threads creeps past two or three.

Keep all of this lightweight — the habits of a considerate colleague, not a checklist recited out loud.
